Chapter II — The Method

How she actually thinks.

Chapter II — Project Deep Dive

The DEKRA chapter: 27 markets, one conclusion.

In 2024, Sanjana joined DEKRA's strategy team in Stuttgart. Her task: analyse the Periodic Technical Inspection (PTI) market for vehicles across 27 countries across the CEEMEA region, and turn it into something actionable.

She didn't just produce a report. She mapped regulatory barriers, identified M&A and joint venture opportunities, ran cross-border workshops with international team members, and monitored risks in DEKRA's existing operations. She built agile project plans in JIRA, coordinated milestones across time zones, and helped set up DEKRA's organisational structure abroad in line with local legislation.

"She didn't just tell us what the market looked like. She told us what to do about it."

— How her manager might describe it

The Eberspächer chapter adds a different flavour: she took a PMO department mid-digital-transformation and helped automate it — creating AI-powered training courses with Synthesia and Articulate that replaced in-person sessions across 7 business units. She tracked 176 programs, maintained master data, supported automated project assessments, and contributed to the monthly CDB rollout.

What connects these stories is not the industries. It's the pattern: she arrives, she understands the system, she improves it.

Chapter II — Working Style

Risk, people, and the space between.

Here is what separates a project coordinator from a project manager: the coordinator tracks what's happening. The manager sees what's coming.

Sanjana has tracked escalations, documented change requests, monitored risk registers, and built mitigation strategies — at DEKRA, at Eberspächer, and at AEMS. But she'll be the first to tell you that none of that means anything if the people in the room don't trust you enough to flag the problem early.

She runs workshops. She translates between technical teams and non-technical stakeholders. She has worked across India, Germany, the UK, North America, Australia, and 27 markets in between — which means she has learned that most problems are not technical. They are human. And she is very good with humans.

Chapter III — The Person

The parts that don't fit on a CV.

She was born in India, studied statistics in Ahmedabad, won a gold medal in an international English olympiad at school, tutored economics students through university, and wrote academic content for the Blind People's Association because someone needed to and she knew how.

Then she moved to Germany. Alone, in a new language, in a country she didn't grow up in — and enrolled in a Masters programme, found work at two major German companies, and became business fluent in a language that is genuinely difficult. She did all of this while cooking elaborate meals, reading voraciously, and occasionally winning at billiards.

Chapter III — Values

What she actually cares about.

She cares about things working. Not in a perfectionist, paralysing way — in the way that a good engineer cares about a bridge: it has to hold, it has to be efficient, and ideally it should be a little bit beautiful.

She cares about people understanding things. This is why she builds training materials, runs workshops, creates dashboards instead of sending raw data. She has a gift for translating complexity into clarity, and she uses it constantly.

She cares about doing something that matters at scale. Working across 27 countries at DEKRA, touching 176 programs at Eberspächer — she is not someone who wants a small life or a small role. She wants to be in the room where the decisions are made, and she wants to be useful there.

"Good project management, like good cooking, is invisible when it works."

— Sanjana Acharya

Chapter III — Off the Clock

The life between the projects.

She reads. Constantly, widely, and with opinions. The fact that this application is structured as a choose-your-own-adventure is not a coincidence — it reflects the way she moves through information: not linearly, but with curiosity and branching interest.

She cooks. And not the way someone cooks because they live alone and have to — the way someone cooks because they enjoy transforming raw ingredients into something greater than the sum of their parts. She sees the parallel herself. She made an entire cookbook out of her career to prove it.

She plays badminton. She plays billiards. She writes. She has spent years helping others — tutoring students, writing for people who couldn't write for themselves, volunteering time before she had much to spare.

She is, in short, the kind of person who makes every room slightly more interesting and every team slightly more capable. You don't find that in the skills section.
